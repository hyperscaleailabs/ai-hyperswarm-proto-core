No human in the loop for the happy path — the theory being that CI is the contract: if it's green, it ships. Over the last five `implement`-kind runs, four failed to reach green. That 80% failure rate, on top of 37 accumulated lessons, is the actual state of the system, not an edge case worth hand-waving.\n\n## What we adopted\n\n**Auto-merge as a hard gate, not a suggestion.** Early versions treated CI red as \"retry later.\" We moved to: red blocks merge indefinitely until a human or a follow-up run investigates. This was the right call — it stopped bad changes from landing by attrition — but it means throughput is now bottlenecked on root-causing failures, not on writing code. Writing code was never the hard part.\n\n**Retrieval-grounded planning.** We required the planner to read and cite its own prior lessons before filing new tickets (rather than synthesizing from vibes). The intent was to stop the loop from re-discovering the same failure mode across cycles. It shipped, but it hasn't yet visibly reduced the fail rate — the four recent failures span registry work, taxonomy/backlog triggers, and the retrieval-grounding change itself, which suggests citation of past lessons isn't sufficient when the failures are heterogeneous rather than repeats of one known bug.\n\n**Failure taxonomy in the ledger.** We started classifying *why* a change failed (not just pass/fail) and using postmortems to trigger backlog items. This is the piece I'd defend most: without it, \"4 fail\" is just a number. With it, we at least have a shape to the failures to design against.\n\n**Pre-PR acceptance audit / diff-hygiene gate.** Added a check before a PR is even opened, to catch hygiene problems earlier than CI. It's the most recent change and hasn't proven itself yet — no signal either way.\n\n## What actually failed\n\nEvery one of the four failing lessons has the identical postmortem line: *\"Change did not reach green; auto-merge will hold until CI passes.\"* That's not a diagnosis, it's a symptom repeated four times. The taxonomy and retrieval-grounding investments were supposed to turn that into something more specific — and they haven't yet. The honest read: we've built good infrastructure for *observing* failure, but the loop still can't reliably produce a change that passes CI on the first or second attempt.
